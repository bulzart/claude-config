## 4b The engagement blueprint (A6s: designed before any line is written)

The user, 2026-11-13: "making the viewer hooked throughout the whole video and engaged, not just facts, if its fact story do not lie, but
also do not be boring too, everything is about making the user attached but yet enjoyable and attractive with being a specialist youtube
content creator and viewer professional user engagement, and attention hooker and also all other fields", and: "this has to be in early
stages of planning and video development, and not just check and edit and do double effort after the whole video story is already crafted
and finished".

So engagement is **designed first and built in**, never a rescue pass over a finished script. The team (§0) writes one file per video,
`40-briefs/video-NN-blueprint.md`, **after the user's subject pick and before any line, image prompt or bed caption exists**. The script
(§4), the image plan (§5), the bed (§6), the moves, joins and sounds of the edit (§7) and the title, thumbnail and description (§12) are
then *made from it*, each taking its part. The judge's checkpoints (§13) confirm that the plan files carry the blueprint (J0 reads the
blueprint itself, before the script); a script written to its blueprint needs no second pass, and a miss a check finds is a small fix to
one plan file, before the generation it guards.

**Where it enters, in order**
1. The pool (A6s step 2, §3): every candidate carries its *story material* (below) and is scored on it; the research collects people,
   moments and specifics beside the claims, so the blueprint's slots have something true to hold.
2. Step 6a: the blueprint, made from the spec's engagement numbers (§2), the pool's story material and the approach recorded for the video
   (§10). A page or two of decisions, not prose.
3. J0 (§13): the judge sets the blueprint against the spec's numbers and edits it. Nothing is generated and no line exists yet.
4. Step 6b: research tops up only the slots the blueprint left empty (the sourcing rule of §3). Step 6c: the script is written beat by
   beat, each beat's lines doing the job the blueprint gave it (`script.md` names the beat over each section).
5. Steps 7-8: each image's `job` and `shot`, its move and join, the labels, the effects and story sounds, the bed's sections, the title,
   the thumbnail and the publishing kit follow the blueprint's beats.

**What the blueprint holds.** Each number is the spec's ("Retention, emotion, language, engagement, attachment, the social side"),
scaled to the video's minutes; a number the spec does not give is the agent's, with its reason.
- **The promise.** One sentence: what the viewer gets and the question they must have answered. The title, the thumbnail and the first
  line make that one promise (§12) and the end keeps it; nothing is promised that the video does not deliver.
- **The hook** (the references' first seconds, §2): a precise moment, the stake or twist that makes it matter now, the promise.
- **The cast.** *You*: the viewer's stand-in, one anchor. A **thread**: a real person or small group, sourced, whose own small arc runs
  beside "you" and shows up at least three times, spaced (planted early, complicated in the middle, paid near the peak): the references'
  recurring character (12 of 16) and side characters with small arcs (the F3 videos). The **crowd**: what the others do and why (real,
  sourced behaviour: who helps, who gives up, who copies whom). The **force** against them (the clock, the cold, the unknown). A private
  person is told by role, never by name (a public figure by name, as the sources hold); where no real thread is sourced, the thread is a
  stand-in framed as one ("picture the driver in the next car"), never told as fact.
- **Stakes and the running number.** What can be lost, and how each chapter raises or flips it; the number the viewer watches move (fuel,
  money, days, warm windows) with its beats.
- **The emotion map.** The mood turns the spec counts (about 7-8 a video), each with its place (a share of the length) and its feeling;
  the lowest point and what lifts it; the main peak at the spec's place (80-96 % of the length in 11 of 16). The feeling is carried by the
  words, the picture's light and the bed, never by a louder voice (§4 "Emotion").
- **The open-loop ledger.** Every question or promise raised, where it is planted and where it is paid: one loop alive at every moment
  after the hook; the first paid inside the first third, the biggest in the last fifth (14 of 16 pay in the last fifth); none dropped,
  none paid at once. A chapter ends on the next problem, a signpost or a counted card.
- **The re-hook rhythm.** A turn at least every 30-60 s (13 of 16; the longest stretch without one 35-100 s, median about 60 s), each a
  named device from the spec ("Here's the problem", "But there's a catch", a cutaway, a number that lands, a question, a contrast, the
  thread), placed on the map and not discovered in the draft. A new picture every 2-5 s holds what lies between.
- **Every fact has a job.** A fact goes in as a *scene* (a person doing or choosing something), a *number at human scale* (said as what the
  viewer can picture), a *turn* (it changes what they thought) or a *tool* (they can use it now, with the reason it matters), and the
  specific beats the general (the half bag of nuts, not "some food"). A fact that is none of these is cut or kept to one line. A run of
  tips never goes past two or three items without a story beat (a person, a choice, a mistake, a number). Reports, agencies, dates and
  policy are told as what they did to a person or mean for the viewer and run about 20 s together at most; the "it happened before" cases
  and the "what changed" part each hold one human detail and one lesson the viewer can use, and never sit right before the payoff.
- **The human layer (the social side).** Where behaviour carries the story: the pull the viewer would feel and why people give in to it,
  the mistake and the better choice, what strangers do for each other, the small shared moments; and why the video would be passed on
  (§1: a surprising fact to repeat, a usable lesson, identity, a harmless debate: 13 of 16). One **debate question**, harmless and
  answerable in a line, is planted at the story's turn and asked at the end.
- **Wit and questions.** The references' register (dry in 9 of 16; questions about 0.19 a minute, mostly self-answered): a few dry lines
  where they come naturally, never at someone's suffering and never inside a strong line; each question placed at a turn.
- **The asks.** What the references do (a subscribe line mid-video in 4 of 16, at 25-61 % of the length; a comment question at the end in
  4; the next video named in 2), chosen for the user's aim, an engaged and returning audience: said once, softly, in the narrator's own
  voice, at a warm moment and never in the middle of a rule or a peak; the debate question and a share line (who would need this) come
  before the closing line, which stays last.
- **The ending.** The big loop paid, the bookend to the hook (13 of 16), the closing line worth repeating, then nothing.
- **The pictures' jobs.** For each beat what its pictures do (establish, escalate, human, reveal, relief, instruct), the shot (wide,
  medium, close, detail, diagram), the light (the emotion map's arc inside the style lock: cold and muted at dread, the one warm accent
  where a human moment or hope arrives), where a face carries the feeling, and the variety rule (no scene or shot more than three stills
  running without a reason). §5 turns it into each image's `job` and `shot`.
- **The sound's jobs.** The bed's sections follow the emotion map (§6); the silence or dip before a reveal; story sounds only where the
  story names a sound (the references' practice; from the user's sounds folder, never a meme or a death sound); the planned push-ins (at
  dread or scale, a beat's first still) and dips (at chapters) sit on the blueprint's beats (§7).
- **The publishing kit** (§12): two or three title options in the recorded title form, the thumbnail concept (the twist in two or three
  words, the references' pattern), the description (its first two lines the promise, then the chapters) and the pinned comment (the
  debate question): one promise in all of them.

**The viewer's-seat test**, once at the end of 6a, on the blueprint, where a change costs a line: read each beat as a stranger on the
feed: what do I want to know next, what do I feel, why would I not leave? A beat with no answer to any is redesigned there.

**The truth rule** (the user, 2026-11-13: "if its fact story do not lie"). Drama comes from the choice, order, focus, framing and pace of
true material, never from an invented event, number, quote or person, a figure made larger, or a fact made softer or louder than its
source. A stand-in is framed as one; a real person's moment has its source in the fact sheet and is told as theirs; what two sources
disagree on is said as a disagreement or left out. The blueprint never leaves §11 and §11b (tension comes from time, cold, uncertainty,
choices and what may happen next, never from gore, agony, a body or a death told) and never leaves the references' register (a calm
documentary stays calm: the ambition sharpens it, it never turns it into clickbait).

**Short form and other families.** A video under three minutes keeps the hook, one thread or one loop, one turn and the ending. A
factual-history or explainer video (§2, F1, F2) maps its own family's beats (the turning points; the steps and the held-back surprise)
in the same places.

**Never:** a blueprint written after the script; a script with a beat the blueprint does not hold; a late "engagement pass" that
rewrites a finished story; a fact kept for being true that does no job; an invented person to attach the viewer to.
